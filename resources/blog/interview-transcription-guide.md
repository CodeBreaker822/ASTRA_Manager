Interview transcription supports research, journalism, hiring, oral histories, investigations, customer discovery, and project documentation. Automatic speech recognition can create the first draft quickly, but the interviewer or reviewer still owns the decisions about consent, accuracy, redaction, and quotation.

Use this workflow to move from an interview recording to text without treating generated output as unquestionable fact.

## 1. Establish consent and purpose

Before recording, explain why the interview is being captured, how the audio and transcript will be used, who can access them, and how long they will be retained. Follow the law, ethics process, contract, or organizational policy that applies to your work.

Do not assume that permission to speak is automatically permission to record, transcribe online, publish, or train another system. Those can be separate decisions.

The transcription mode matters. Hosted transcription sends the required audio to online services. Offline mode in the JERVA Windows app runs selected Whisper transcription on the local computer after the model is installed. Choose the route allowed by your project.

## 2. Record intelligible speech

Place the microphone between the interviewer and participant, away from noise and vibration. A quiet room and a nearby microphone usually improve results more than changing file formats later.

Ask one question at a time. Avoid speaking over the participant, especially at the beginning or end of an important answer. If a response includes an unfamiliar person, place, or technical term, ask for the spelling when appropriate.

Monitor the recording long enough to confirm both voices are present. Keep notes about key moments, but do not let typing or paper movement dominate the audio.

## 3. Preserve and label the source

Store the unchanged recording with a project identifier and date. Avoid putting unnecessary personal information in the filename. Keep a separate participant key when anonymization is required.

Listen to samples from the beginning, middle, and end before transcription. Confirm the file is complete and that the correct interview was selected.

JERVA accepts common recordings such as MP3, WAV, M4A, AAC, OGG, FLAC, and WebM. Learn the practical differences in [audio file formats for transcription](/blog/audio-file-formats-for-transcription).

## 4. Produce the first-draft transcript

Use JERVA's browser workspace for hosted processing or the Windows app for online or supported offline Whisper transcription. Longer interviews are split into sections so you can follow progress, retry a failed range, and review text alongside timing and available playback.

If you know the interview language and the interface offers a matching selection, choose it carefully. Code-switching, short answers, dialect, and closely related languages can still challenge automatic detection.

Do not infer quality from processing speed alone. A transcript that appears quickly still needs review.

## 5. Choose a transcription style

Decide what the project needs before editing.

### Full verbatim

Includes repetitions, false starts, filler words, and relevant non-speech events. This can be useful when speech patterns matter, but automatic output will not capture every hesitation consistently.

### Clean verbatim

Removes distracting fillers and repairs obvious grammar while preserving meaning. This is common for readable interviews, but editors must avoid making the participant sound more certain or polished than they were.

### Edited extract

Uses selected passages or quotations rather than a complete transcript. Every retained quotation should be checked against the recording and placed in fair context.

JERVA's raw output is a starting point. Online polish can assist readability, but it does not decide the correct editorial standard for your project.

## 6. Review systematically

First confirm completeness and order. Then replay:

- The participant's name or assigned identifier
- Names of other people and organizations
- Dates, amounts, percentages, and measurements
- Technical and local terminology
- Emotional or ambiguous statements
- Negations that change meaning
- Every quotation planned for publication

Speaker grouping can help distinguish turns, but labels are detected voice groups rather than known identities. Verify them before replacing a generic label with a person's name.

Use brackets or a consistent marker for genuinely inaudible wording instead of inventing a sentence that fits the context.

## 7. Redact and protect the transcript

Searchable text can expose sensitive details quickly. Apply the agreed anonymization or redaction process to names, contact details, health information, financial data, locations, or other protected information.

Keep the original, working copy, and redacted copy separate. Record who performed the review when the project requires an audit trail. Restrict access to exported files as well as the source audio.

## 8. Export for analysis or publication

Choose TXT for portable text, a Word-compatible file for editing and comments, or an Excel-compatible file when sections need to be coded or organized in rows.

For qualitative research, your analysis method may require line numbers, participant identifiers, or coding columns that are added after export. For publication, work from the reviewed text and replay each selected quote one final time.

## Interview transcript quality checklist

- Recording and processing consent are documented.
- The selected online or offline mode matches project policy.
- The source file is complete and preserved.
- The transcription style is defined.
- Names, figures, terminology, and quotations were checked.
- Inaudible text is marked rather than guessed.
- Speaker identities are verified.
- Required redaction and access controls are applied.
